## 1. Nvidia Ousts Jensen Huang After Spy Scandal and Affairs

*Wall Street Journal · 12 min read · Lead*

Nvidia's board has forced out founder and CEO Jensen Huang after a cascade of revelations about two extramarital affairs, one with a woman identified by U.S. intelligence as a Chinese intelligence asset. The Wall Street Journal reported on November 13 that the board is preparing to oust Huang after learning of an affair with Nvidia VP of Healthcare Kimberly Powell, who reports to him, and a separate relationship with Alice Huang (no relation), founder of Megaspeed, a company that allegedly smuggled Nvidia chips to China. The New York Times confirmed the Powell affair on November 11, and the Washington Post reported on November 13 that Senator Marco Rubio used a CIA report about Alice Huang to pressure the administration. Huang resigned on November 13; CFO Colette Kress was named interim CEO.

The scale of the fallout is staggering. Nvidia held $48 billion in commitments to 229 private companies as of July and offered $165 billion in cloud backstops, according to the New York Times. The Wall Street Journal reported that Nvidia's $500 billion lending consortium is now in flux, with at least one lender potentially exiting. Bloomberg reported that U.S. scrutiny imperils $3.2 billion in Chinese AI projects reliant on Nvidia GPUs. The Financial Times revealed that Huang struggled to gain access to Beijing policymakers driving China's chip self-sufficiency campaign despite 20+ years of business there. Semafor detailed how Nvidia used a "scorched earth, adversarial attitude" targeting export-control proponents in DC, invoking Trump's name to expedite licenses.

Who gains: The board has reportedly approached Arm CEO Rene Haas as a potential successor (Financial Times). Semafor named Apple hardware chief Johny Srouji as another candidate. Interim CEO Colette Kress, a 13-year Microsoft veteran, takes the helm during the search. Who loses: Nvidia's stock dropped 5%+ pre-market on the ouster news; the company's sprawling startup ecosystem faces uncertainty; U.S.-China chip policy is now entangled with a personal scandal. What to watch: The board's search timeline, whether the DOJ investigation into Megaspeed expands to Nvidia, and whether the U.S. tightens chip export rules further — Politico reported the affair "significantly raised the chances" of Congress enacting limits via the NDAA. Unclear: The full scope of Huang's involvement in Megaspeed's chip diversion, whether other board members knew, and the impact on Nvidia's China revenue (roughly 20% of sales).

### 3. FAA Expands AI-Powered SMART System to All 22 Air Route Centers Nationwide

*Ars Technica · 6 min read · Core*

The FAA announced on November 10 it is expanding SMART (Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories) from the Washington, DC region to all 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers across the U.S. The $875 million, 12-year contract with Air Space Intelligence went live in the National Capital Region on September 21. The FAA says SMART has rerouted 180 flights since then, saving an average of 10 minutes and nearly 80 gallons of fuel per flight. SMART ingests ~200 data streams — weather, flight paths, traffic flow, controller staffing — to predict congestion and recommend schedule changes. A DC-based team validates recommendations before they reach local controllers, who retain final authority.

Significance: This is the first nationwide deployment of AI-driven air traffic planning in the U.S. Airlines (Delta, American) supported the rollout, but the National Air Traffic Controllers Association (15,000+ members) said it was not consulted on design, testing, or implementation. House Democrats have asked the GAO to audit SMART's strategy, metrics, and safety risk management. The expansion came just days before a nationwide ground stop on November 13 caused by a communications network update — the FAA and DHS say no link to SMART. What to watch: Controller adoption rates, independent verification of fuel/time savings, GAO audit findings, and whether SMART's predictive layer integrates with the broader FMDS modernization platform. Unclear: Whether the nationwide rollout timeline was accelerated by political pressure, and if the ground stop exposed gaps SMART was meant to catch.

**Verdict:** Read in full — a landmark AI deployment in critical infrastructure with union and congressional pushback. [Read the original](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2026/11/faa-expands-ai-advice-to-manage-air-traffic-congestion-across-entire-us-airspace/)

### 5. OpenAI Launches ChatGPT Wallet, Letting "Dots" Agents Make Purchases for Users

What to watch: Merchant adoption and agent-compatibility fixes, whether United/Amazon hold their blocks, expansion beyond Pro/US, and if transaction fees become a revenue line. Unclear: Per-purchase spending caps, dispute resolution flow for agent-made purchases, and whether "pre-approve" delegation parameters (spending limits, categories, time windows) arrive as promised.

### 7. EU Proposes Digital Fairness Act Targeting Dark Patterns, Addictive Design, and Influencer Ads

*European Commission / Osborne Clarke · 6 min read · Need to Know*

The European Commission unveiled its Digital Fairness Act (DFA) proposal on November 11, a regulation replacing the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive to address manipulative online practices. Key provisions: a ban on 10 blacklisted "dark patterns" (fake scarcity, persistent prompts, inferred consent, hidden cancellation paths, fake notifications); a "fairness by design" obligation; a consumer right to disable addictive features (infinite scroll, autoplay, loot boxes, streaks, variable rewards, engagement-optimized recommenders, push notifications timed for re-engagement); mandatory influencer ad labeling ("advertisement" or "commercial collaboration"); transparent pricing (ban on drip pricing); and simplified contract cancellation. The regulation applies to all consumer-facing businesses, not just digital platforms.

Significance: A 2022 Commission study found 97% of top websites/apps used dark patterns. The DFA goes beyond the DSA and GDPR by targeting design-level manipulation directly. Critics (BEUC, digital rights groups) say it doesn't go far enough; industry lawyers (Taylor Wessing) argue much is already covered by UCPD, DSA, CRD, and enforcement is the real gap. The proposal must pass Parliament and Council — likely 2027+ before enforcement.

What to watch: Legislative amendments (especially on "addictive design" definition), lobbying from platforms and ad-tech, interplay with DSA enforcement, and whether the "right to disable" becomes a global standard. Unclear: How "addictive design" is legally distinguished from "engaging design," the compliance burden for non-EU companies targeting EU users, and enforcement resourcing.

**Verdict:** Read in full — the most comprehensive regulatory attempt yet to govern digital interface design, with global precedent potential. [Read the original](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_2407)
